# Slide craft: turning a presenter outline into excellent slides

Primary-source review, 15 October 2026, for the `adk-presenter` skill. This
note covers the physical slide: layout, text, charts, diagrams, colour and
rendering. Deck structure, action titles, SCQA, Zelazny's chart choice,
Duarte's five-minute path, Tufte's *Cognitive Style of PowerPoint* and the
decision slide are in [stakeholder-presentations.md](stakeholder-presentations.md)
and are not repeated. Pages can change after the date read.

## Conclusion

The craft rules converge on one slide shape: a full-sentence headline that
states the claim, one piece of visual evidence under it, and almost nothing
else. It is the only slide design with a controlled comparison behind it
(Garner and Alley, 2011) and is what Mayer's coherence, signalling and
redundancy findings predict. Practitioners add the test and the method: the
point should land in about three seconds; remove everything that does not
carry the message, then use one colour and one annotation to point at the
pattern. For agent results, show the baseline on the result chart, state
interval and sample in a caption, use bars and lines on a shared baseline, and
never a pie, a secondary axis or 3D. For architecture, use a legend, label
every arrow, draw trust boundaries, and reveal in layers. The slide carries the
claim and the evidence; the notes carry the argument; the linked document
carries the detail. Where the project has no number, the headline says "not
measured".

## What the sources actually establish

### 1. Alley: assertion-evidence slides, and the one controlled test

Penn State's instruction set prescribes: "Place a one-sentence assertion as
the headline of each new slide. The assertion should be no more than two
lines", "Provide visual evidence ... on each slide. Avoid bullet points",
"Restrict yourself to one or two call-outs per page", "Use 28 point type for
the headlines, 18-24 point type for the body text", "Keep lists to no more
than four items". Garner, Alley, Sawarynski, Wolfe and Zappe (ASEE 2011)
showed two audiences, 55 and 56 students, the same six-minute recorded talk on
MRI tumour detection with topic-subtopic versus assertion-evidence slides,
tested immediately and a week later. "On the questions testing for
comprehension and retention of more complex concepts, students learning from
the assertion-evidence slides scored higher"; some differences reached
significance, and the one reverse result was immediate recall of statistics
printed only on the bullet slides (69 vs 39 percent). Bullet slides averaged
30.4 words. The title says "appear to": one study, one topic, students.
[Instruction set](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.psu.edu/dist/7/13153/files/2008/10/Assertion-Evidence-Slides-Instruction_Set.pdf),
[Garner et al. 2011](https://peer.asee.org/17510), read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** headline is a sentence of at most two lines; body is one
visual; at most two call-outs; no bullets on body slides. A number the audience
must recall exactly (threshold, cost) is written on the slide, since that is
the one thing bullet slides did better.

### 2. Mayer: coherence, signalling, redundancy, contiguity

Mayer's chapter in the *Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning* (2005)
states: coherence, "People learn more deeply from a multimedia message when
extraneous material is excluded rather than included" (10 of 11 experiments,
median effect size 1.32); signalling, cues "that highlight the organization of
the essential material" (3 of 3, 0.60); redundancy, "People learn more deeply
from graphics and narration than from graphics, narration, and on-screen text"
(10 of 10, 0.69); spatial contiguity, words and pictures "near rather than far
from each other" (8 of 8, 1.11). The 2014 edition reports coherence at 23 of
23 and redundancy at 16 of 16, both median 0.86. Laboratory lessons with
retention tests, not meetings; redundancy concerns text duplicating narration,
and brief labels on a graphic are reported to help.
[Handbook chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/C98AB3A6CE760DD63C048936EA0B3B44),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** the spoken script never appears on the slide; labels and the
takeaway annotation stay, paragraphs go to the notes. Labels sit next to what
they label, not in a legend. Decorative images and repeated logos are
extraneous material.

### 3. Kosslyn: what real decks get wrong

Kosslyn, Kievit, Russell and Shephard (*Frontiers in Psychology*, 2012) scored
140 randomly sampled decks against 137 rules. Under strict scoring every deck
violated discriminability and limited capacity; the commonest failures were
"More than two lines are used per bulleted sentence" (91 percent), "More than
four bulleted items appear in a single list" (91 percent), all-caps or
all-italic text (81 percent), and "Words are not large enough (i.e., greater
than 20 point)" (66 percent). A prevalence survey, not an outcome study.
[Kosslyn et al. 2012](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00230/full),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** at least 20 point for anything the audience must read; no
all-caps; any unavoidable list has at most four items of two lines.

### 4. Reynolds and Duarte: signal, the glance test, the slidedoc

Reynolds: "Nothing in your slide should be superfluous"; "Every element in a
design should be expressed to the extent necessary, but not beyond"; design for
the back of the room; "declarative titles" on charts; no "slideuments".
Duarte's glance test asks "Will your audience understand the main point of
your slide in 3 seconds or less?" Her 6 September 2019 post separates
"cinematic slides" with "low-to-no text" from a slidedoc, "a document that
combines visuals and text in a succinct and meaningful way, so the key
messages are easily understood without the need of a presenter", built from
the notes view. Practitioner prescriptions.
[Reynolds](https://garrreynolds.com/design-tips),
[Glance test](https://www.duarte.com/resources/guides-tools/the-glance-test/),
[Slidedoc](https://www.duarte.com/blog/the-slides-you-deliver-versus-the-slidedoc-you-leave-behind/),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** one outline, two renderings: the projected deck (headline,
evidence, caption) and the leave-behind (same slides with the notes as body
text). Never one deck that tries to be both.

### 5. Knaflic and Ajani: declutter, then focus

Knaflic's method: "get rid of the visual elements in your graphs that are
unnecessary", then find the "so what?" and use "words and color to make the
point clear". Ajani, Lee, Xiong, Knaflic, Kemper and Franconeri (IEEE TVCG,
accepted 2021) tested six graph topics in cluttered, decluttered and
decluttered-plus-focused versions: "Decluttering designs led to higher ratings
on professionalism, and adding focus to the design led to higher ratings on
aesthetics and clarity. They also showed better memory for the highlighted
pattern in the data", with the caveat "we do not know whether these results
would generalize beyond our memory-based tasks". Focus means "a clear headline
that describes the relevant data pattern, highlighting a subset of relevant
data values with a unique color, and connecting those values to written
annotations".
[Declutter post, 29 March 2017](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2017/3/29/declutter-this-graph),
[Ajani et al.](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10350268), read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** charts are grey by default; the bar or series carrying the
claim is the one coloured element; the takeaway is written on the chart beside
it in words that match the headline.

### 6. Tufte: data-ink

Tufte (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, 1983) defines the
data-ink ratio as the share of ink that displays data, and chartjunk as
decoration or "computer debris" that makes a graphic "purvey Graphical Style
rather than quantitative information". A principle, not a measurement; the
Ajani study is the nearest evidence.
[Summary](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/chart-junk-and-data-ink-origins),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** no 3D, shadows, plot backgrounds or empty axis lines.

### 7. Few and Knaflic: charts to avoid

Few, "Save the Pies for Dessert" (August 2007): the pie "is by far the least
effective" graph; slices read accurately only "close to 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, or
100%", while a bar with a percentage scale compares magnitudes far better.
Few, "Dual-Scaled Axes in Graphs" (March 2008): "I can't think of a single
case when there isn't a better solution than a graph with a dual-scaled
axis"; use stacked panels sharing an x-axis. Knaflic (25 May 2011): with a
secondary axis "it's not always clear which data series belongs to which
axis"; label directly or split vertically. Her book rules out 3D because it
"skews our numbers".
[Few, pies](http://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/visual_business_intelligence/save_the_pies_for_dessert.pdf),
[Few, dual axes](http://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/visual_business_intelligence/dual-scaled_axes.pdf),
[Knaflic](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2011/05/secondary-y-axis),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** before/after against baseline is two bars on one scale, or a
line with the baseline as a flat reference; cost per unit and volume are two
stacked panels on one time axis. The renderer refuses pie, donut, dual axis
and 3D.

### 8. Uncertainty on the chart

Datawrapper (18 October 2021) draws a confidence interval as a range overlay
and names it in the title key because "there are different kinds of confidence
interval (90%, 95%)". The data.europa.eu guide cites readers who "incorrectly
believed that the limits of the confidence interval represented minimum and
maximum forecasted temperatures" and prefers showing the underlying points.
[Datawrapper](https://www.datawrapper.de/academy/confidence-intervals-error-bars-datawrapper-bar-charts),
[Error bars](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/error-bars-and-the-cone-of-uncertainty),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** every result chart carries a small caption: "n = 412
conversations, 3 runs, 95 percent interval, 2 to 20 September, measured by
<method>". Draw the interval as a light range and name it; with small n show
the points, not a bar.

### 9. Funnels

Knaflic (3 June 2024): funnel shapes "are center-aligned. This makes
accurately comparing the length or area of each section difficult to do since
the bars don't share a consistent baseline."
[SWD funnel challenge](https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/swdchallenge-funnel-chart),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** a funnel (cases received, handled by the agent, resolved
without escalation, accepted) is left-aligned horizontal bars in stage order
with the step conversion written between bars.

### 10. Kawasaki: 10/20/30

Kawasaki (30 December 2005): "a PowerPoint presentation should have ten
slides, last no more than twenty minutes, and contain no font smaller than
thirty points", because large type forces presenters to "find the most salient
points". A pitch opinion, consistent with Alley's 28 point and Kosslyn's 20
point floor.
[Kawasaki](https://guykawasaki.com/the_102030_rule/), read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** ten slides is the target for the business deck's main path;
30 point is the headline size.

### 11. Accessibility: contrast and colour

WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.3 requires text contrast "of at least 4.5:1", or "at least
3:1" for large text, "at least 18 point or 14 point bold"; SC 1.4.6 raises
this to 7:1. Wong (*Nature Methods*, June 2011) reports about 8 percent of men
and 0.5 percent of women with red-green colour blindness and recommends the
Okabe and Ito palette (#E69F00, #56B4E9, #009E73, #F0E442, #0072B2, #D55E00,
#CC79A7, black) with redundant encoding such as labels. The Okabe and Ito
page returned HTTP 403.
[WCAG](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html),
[Wong 2011](https://www.nature.com/articles/nmeth.1618), read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** 4.5:1 for text and marks; emphasis colour from Okabe-Ito or
the brand's accessible equivalent; never red versus green for baseline versus
agent; readable in greyscale; alt text on every image where the host allows.

### 12. Architecture diagrams: C4, trust boundaries, Google's reference

C4's notation page: "Every diagram should have a title describing the diagram
type and scope", "a key/legend explaining the notation", "Every line should be
labelled", "Every container and component should have a technology explicitly
specified", colour coding "consistent (within and across diagrams)". Its
context, container and component levels are the progressive reveal.
Microsoft's threat-modelling guide draws trust boundaries as dotted lines "to
show where different entities are in control" on a data-flow diagram.
Google's single-agent reference architecture (updated 9 December 2025) groups
frontend, ADK agent on Cloud Run, Gemini, MCP tools, session storage,
observability and IAM, and recommends Agent Identity with "each agent only the
permissions that it needs". OWASP's *Agentic AI Threats and Mitigations* v1.0
(17 February 2025) is the threat catalogue the boundaries map to.
[C4](https://c4model.com/diagrams/notation),
[Microsoft DFD](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/security/develop/threat-modeling-tool-getting-started),
[Google](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/single-agent-ai-system-adk-cloud-run),
[OWASP ASI](https://genai.owasp.org/initiatives/agentic-ai-threats-and-mitigations/),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** at most three diagrams: context (users, agent, external
systems); container (agent, model, tools, session and memory stores, identity
on each arrow, trust boundaries as dotted regions); a component view only where
a contract is being asked for. Same shapes, colours and legend throughout;
every arrow names protocol and identity; the design document is linked, not
redrawn.

### What the sources do not establish

No source tests a words-per-slide limit; the figures below derive from
Alley's two-line headline and two call-outs, Kosslyn's four-by-two lists and
the 30-word bullet slides that did not help. No source prescribes how to show
"not measured"; our rule follows the Columbia case in the earlier note. No
source measures brand templates; C4 consistency and WCAG apply to whatever
template the company supplies. The executive summary, appendix and one-slide
version follow Duarte's 10 percent rule and Minto in the earlier note.

## Per-slide checklist

Apply before rendering each body slide; fail any item and rewrite.

1. Headline is a complete sentence stating a claim, at most two lines, and
   someone could disagree with it.
2. One message: the slide supports that sentence and nothing else.
3. Evidence is visible (chart, diagram, table, screenshot, quoted test
   result), not described in bullets.
4. The takeaway is annotated on the chart in the headline's words; one
   emphasis colour; everything else grey.
5. Method and sample in small text: source, n, repeats, interval, dates, who
   measured. Projections say "estimate"; untested claims say "hypothesis".
6. "Not measured" is in the headline when the record lacks the number; no
   placeholder figures.
7. Word budget: headline at most 20 words; body at most 40 words including
   call-outs and caption; no list longer than four items of two lines.
8. Type: headline 28 to 30 point, body at least 20 point, caption at least
   14 point; no all-caps or all-italic.
9. Contrast at least 4.5:1; colour-blind safe palette; readable in
   greyscale; alt text for images.
10. Units consistent across the deck (one currency, one time unit, one
    definition of "case"), stated on the axis.
11. Chart form matches the comparison: bars or lines on a shared baseline;
    baseline drawn on the result chart; no pie, donut, dual axis or 3D.
12. Diagram has title, legend, arrows labelled with protocol and identity,
    trust boundaries, and the same notation as the other diagrams.
13. Nothing decorative: logo only on title and closing slides; no stock
    images or transitions.
14. Three-second test: a reader who sees only this slide gets the claim.

## Rendering from the Markdown outline

The action title becomes the headline verbatim. The evidence citation becomes
the visual plus a caption naming the source file, table or evaluation run. The
speaker notes become the notes field, never body text; any sentence in them
the audience must remember exactly (threshold, cost, date, the decision asked)
is promoted to a single call-out. Anything longer than two lines that is not
the headline moves to the notes or the linked document.

Charts come from recorded project data, never typed by hand, each captioned
with n, method and date and annotated with the headline's claim. Diagrams
follow the C4 levels and share one legend. The deck renders twice: projected
(headline, visual, caption) and leave-behind (the same slides with the notes
beside the visual, Duarte's slidedoc). The appendix holds the criteria table,
evaluation excerpts, cost workings and the Q&A bank. The one-slide version is
the executive summary alone and passes the same checklist.
