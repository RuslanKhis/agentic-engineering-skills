# The spoken presentation: speech craft for an AI project's three decisions

Primary-source review, 15 October 2026, for the `adk-presenter` skill's speaker
notes and Q&A bank. Pages can change after the date read; books are cited by
edition where the text is not online. This note covers the speech, not the
slides. Minto/SCQA, action titles, Duarte's five-minute path and Big Idea,
options with a recommendation, RAPID, the premortem and the three voices are in
[stakeholder-presentations.md](stakeholder-presentations.md).

## Conclusion

State the case, then prove it; the rest is optional (Aristotle). Open with the
bottom line and the ask, move the listener between the costly present and the
credible future, end on the decision (BLUF, Monroe, Duarte). Give three
reasons; a fourth raises scepticism when the audience knows you want something
(Shu and Carlson). Say what did not work and answer it: refutational two-sided
messages beat one-sided ones and raise credibility (O'Keefe). Uncertainty as a
number costs almost no trust; vague hedging costs more (van der Bles).
Rehearsal out loud before people correlates with rated performance, and "I am
excited" beats "calm down" (Menzel and Carrell; Brooks). Delivery folklore is
weaker than it sounds: 7-38-55 does not apply to content, "uh" helps listeners,
and forced eye contact can reduce persuasion of people who disagree. The demo
is a recording of the measured build, and every AI claim is one the project
record can substantiate (FTC).

## What the sources actually establish

### 1. Aristotle: state, prove, and the speaker's character

*Rhetoric* I.2: persuasion comes from "the speaker's personal character when
the speech is so spoken as to make us think him credible", from stirring the
hearers' emotions, and from proof in the speech itself; character "may almost
be called the most effective means of persuasion he possesses", and it is
built inside the speech. III.13: "the only necessary parts of a speech are the
Statement and the Argument"; at most "Introduction, Statement, Argument, and
Epilogue". Prescription, not experiment.
[I.2](https://kairos.technorhetoric.net/stasis/2017/honeycutt/aristotle/rhet1-2.html),
[III.13](https://kairos.technorhetoric.net/stasis/2017/honeycutt/aristotle/rhet3-13.html),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** every speech has a one-sentence Statement (the decision and
why) and an Argument (the evidence), with a short proem and epilogue. Ethos is
earned in the talk by method, sample sizes and admitted failures, not by title.

### 2. Monroe's motivated sequence

Alan H. Monroe (Purdue, *Principles and Types of Speech*, 1935 onward):
Attention, Need, Satisfaction, Visualization ("how their lives will change for
the better if the plan is implemented"), Action. A teaching pattern with no
outcome study attached.
[UCCS handout](https://wcc.uccs.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/The%20Five%20Steps%20of%20Monroe%E2%80%99s%20Motivated%20Sequence.pdf),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** the production-approval speech is Monroe in order: the user's
pain today, the measured POC as satisfaction, the production day as
visualization, the decision as action.

### 3. Duarte: "what is" and "what could be"

In her TEDxEast talk and *Resonate* (Wiley, 2010), Duarte's sparkline shows
great talks (King's "I have a dream", Jobs's 2007 iPhone launch) oscillating
between "what is" and "what could be", ending on the "new bliss". This is her
reading of chosen talks, not a controlled comparison; the transcript itself
could not be fetched.
[TED talk](https://www.ted.com/talks/nancy_duarte_the_secret_structure_of_great_talks),
[practitioner summary](https://architectelevator.com/strategy/presenting-waveform/),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** the body alternates one paragraph of "today" (the recorded
baseline) with one of "after" (the measured or estimated result), never more
than two in a row of either, and the close describes the new normal.

### 4. The story spine

Kenn Adams created the eight-line spine in the late 1980s: "Once upon a time
/ Every day / But one day / Because of that (x3) / Until finally / And ever
since then"; it reached Pixar through Rebecca Stockley. An exercise, not
evidence.
[NPR interview, 23 June 2026](https://www.kunc.org/2026-06-23/meet-the-creator-of-the-story-spine-an-8-sentence-tool-to-create-and-analyze-stories),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** the single real case in every speech is told on the spine:
the user's ordinary day, the day the agent was switched on, three consequences
with numbers, and what is now routine.

### 5. Chris Anderson: one idea, built from the familiar

Anderson's TED2016 talk: "Your main goal is to transfer an extraordinary gift,
an idea, into your listeners' minds"; limit the talk to one major idea; give
"a reason to care by sparking curiosity"; "build your idea using concepts
already familiar to your audience"; make it memorable with examples. *TED
Talks* (Houghton Mifflin Harcourt, 2016) calls the connecting thread the
throughline. Curatorial judgement, not measurement.
[Talk summary](https://videohighlight.com/v/-FOCpMAww28), read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** write the throughline as one sentence before any notes; explain
the agent with the company's own vocabulary (the ticket, the claim, the order),
never with model internals.

### 6. Gallo: eighteen minutes, three points, 190 words a minute

*Talk Like TED* (St. Martin's, 2014) prescribes 18 minutes and three main
points, and reports Bryan Stevenson at about 190 words per minute. Gallo's
readings of TED talks, not controlled findings; the 190 figure is one speaker.
[Inc. review on Gallo's site](https://www.carminegallo.com/how-to-give-the-speech-of-a-lifetime-inc-com-book-review/),
[Stevenson figure](https://www.slidegenius.com/blog/storytelling-works-for-many-ted-speakers),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** prepared text at 140 to 160 words per minute (the roughly 150
wpm conversational rate cited by the National Center for Voice and Speech) so
that a 15-minute slot is about 1,500 spoken words leaving room for questions.

### 7. Rule of three, measured

Shu and Carlson, "When Three Charms but Four Alarms", *Journal of Marketing*
78(1), 2014: when the audience knows the source "has a persuasion motive",
impressions rise up to three positive claims and fall at the fourth, when
"persuasion knowledge causes them to view all the claims with skepticism"; the
peak does not appear for sources without a motive. Lab experiments on consumer
messages.
[Abstract via Marginal Revolution](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2014/05/make-three-claims-when-trying-to-persuade.html),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** three reasons, spoken as three; further evidence is held for
Q&A. A presenter asking for budget is a source with a motive.

### 8. Two-sided messages and credibility

O'Keefe's meta-analysis (*Communication Yearbook* 22, 1999; 107 studies,
20,111 participants): refutational two-sided messages are more persuasive than
one-sided, which beat non-refutational two-sided. Raising the counter-argument
and answering it also raises perceived credibility.
[Summary](https://www.spring.org.uk/?p=12521), read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** every speech has a "what did not work and what we did about
it" section before the ask; a failure named without a response is the weakest
option.

### 9. Signposting

Titsworth and Kiewra, *Contemporary Educational Psychology* 29 (2004): a
2x2x2 experiment, 223 participants, scripted video lectures; spoken
organizational cues (advance organizers, transitions, summaries) increased
what students recorded and recalled. "Tell them what you will tell them" is
attributed to Dale Carnegie without a locatable primary text.
[Study record](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/dissertations/AAI9942159),
[Carnegie attribution](https://www.genardmethod.com/blog/bid/192061/how-to-open-a-presentation-tell-em-what-you-re-going-to-say),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** signpost with claims ("second, the cost per case") not labels
("next, the cost slide"). The preview fails when it is a table of contents
rather than the answer, and when it delays the decision past the first minute.

### 10. BLUF

US Army Regulation 25-50 requires correspondence that is "concise, organized,
and to the point" with the "Bottom Line Up Front"; the Air Force *Tongue and
Quill* teaches the same. A writing standard, adopted for briefings.
[AR 25-50 summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Army_Regulation_25-50),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** the first spoken sentence is the decision requested and the
one number that justifies it; context comes second.

### 11. Wes Kao on presenting to a CEO

"How to present to your CEO" (10 January 2024): "you should actually
encourage them to interrupt you"; "keep the backstory short"; open with "1-2
sentences on why you're meeting"; know your numbers; spend "20% on the past
and focus 80% on what's ahead"; "the point is to get to the right decision".
Practitioner advice.
[Newsletter](https://newsletter.weskao.com/p/how-to-present-to-your-ceo),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** invite interruption in the opening; when interrupted, answer
the question in one sentence, then ask "shall I go to the decision now?"
rather than returning to the script.

### 12. Uncertainty does not cost much trust if stated as a number

van der Bles, van der Linden, Freeman and Spiegelhalter, *PNAS* 117(14), 2020:
five experiments including a preregistered replication and a BBC News field
experiment, n = 5,780; "only a small decrease in trust in numbers and
trustworthiness of the source, and mostly for verbal uncertainty
communication". Numeric ranges did not reduce trust in the source.
[PMC copy](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7149229/),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** say "resolution rate 61 percent, range 55 to 67 on 120 cases",
never "roughly works most of the time". Bad news follows the same form: "we
measured X; it did not meet the threshold; here is the cause and the change".

### 13. Nerves: reappraise as excitement

Brooks, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 143(3), 2014: across
karaoke, public speaking and maths tasks, participants who said "I am excited"
rather than "I am calm" performed better and adopted an "opportunity
mind-set". Lab studies with students.
[PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24364682/), read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** the rehearsal checklist tells the speaker to say "I am
excited" before entering, not to calm down.

### 14. Rehearsal correlates with performance

Menzel and Carrell, *Communication Education* 43(1), 1994: rated speech quality
correlated positively with total preparation time, time preparing a visual
aid, "number of rehearsals for an audience", time rehearsing silently and out
loud, and number of rehearsals out loud. Correlational, student speakers.
[ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?q=Menzel+Carrell+%22preparation+and+performance%22+public+speaking),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** at least two full out-loud runs, one before a colleague who
plays the decider and interrupts.

### 15. Delivery folklore versus evidence

Mehrabian's own site: "unless a communicator is talking about their feelings
or attitudes, these equations are not applicable". Fox Tree, *Memory &
Cognition* 29(2), 2001: listeners recognised words faster after "uh"; "um"
neither helped nor hindered. Chen, Minson, Schöne and Heinrichs,
*Psychological Science* 2013: more eye contact "predicts less attitude change
in the direction advocated"; instructed gaze at the eyes persuaded less than
gaze at the mouth. Beebe (1974) had found eye contact raised credibility
ratings. Pace and vocal variety have no primary study we could locate.
[Mehrabian](https://www.kaaj.com/psych/smorder.html),
[Fox Tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_E._Fox_Tree),
[Chen et al.](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797613491968/),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** never claim "93 percent is non-verbal"; reduce fillers by
pausing; spread eye contact across the room rather than holding a sceptic's
gaze while arguing against their view.

### 16. Q&A craft

MIT Sloan 15.281, "Handling Hostility" (29 February 2016): "allow person time
to finish asking question", "restate, paraphrase, clarify", "empathize",
"remove anonymity", "answer honestly", "look at entire audience, not just
questioner". Toastmasters (Windingland, March 2021): pause "a few beats";
"state your point, give a reason, provide an example or evidence, then restate
your point"; "I don't know, but I'll find out and let you know"; save the
concluding words for after the Q&A. Course notes and an article, not studies.
[MIT OCW](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-281-advanced-communication-for-leaders-spring-2016/resources/mit15_281s16_lec8),
[Toastmasters](https://toastmasters.org/magazine/magazine-issues/2021/mar/handling-the-qanda-session-with-confidence),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** see the protocol below. The close is spoken after Q&A.

### 17. Demo discipline

Zach Holman: "Live demos are like Global Thermonuclear War: the only way to
win is to not do a live demo"; use a screencast, or paste from a script.
Lorna Mitchell (2017): "record a (silent) screencast", keep "clearly labelled
videos of all the demos" open as Plan B; "nobody wants to watch you type".
Practitioner rules.
[speaking.io](https://speaking.io/prep/live-demos/),
[lornajane.net](https://lornajane.net/posts/2017/live-demo-risks-and-rewards),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** the demo is a recording of the build that produced the
numbers, labelled with build and date, narrated live; a live run only in a
technical session, with the recording as fallback.

### 18. FTC on AI claims

Michael Atleson, FTC, "Keep your AI claims in check" (27 February 2023): AI
"is a marketing term"; "false or unsubstantiated claims about a product's
efficacy are our bread and butter"; ask whether you exaggerate what it can do,
claim it beats a non-AI product without evidence, know the risks, and use AI
at all. Marketing guidance, applied here by analogy to internal claims.
[Coverage with quotes](https://www.marketingdive.com/news/ftc-AI-warning-OpenAI-ChatGPT-advertising/643739/),
read 15 October 2026 (the ftc.gov URL returned 404).

**Apply here:** banned words in notes unless backed by a recorded measurement:
"revolutionary", "understands", "intelligent", "eliminates", "always". Risk
controls are spoken as confidence builders: "it cannot write to the ledger; a
person approves every refund above X; here is the test that proves it".

### What the sources do not establish

No study compares speech structures for business decisions. Vocal variety,
pause length and an optimal words-per-minute figure have no primary evidence we
could find. The user-day story, credit to the team and the counterfactual are
our rules, consistent with Duarte, Adams and O'Keefe.

## Speech skeletons

Angle brackets are values from the project record; a missing value is spoken
as "not yet measured". Timings are minutes for 15 and 30 minute slots; the
longer slot adds Q&A and case time, never more claims.

### POC approval (15: 10 talk + 5 Q&A; 30: 18 talk + 12 Q&A)

1. BLUF (1 / 1.5): "I am asking for <budget> and <weeks> to test whether an
   agent can cut <metric> from <baseline> by <hypothesis>. Interrupt me."
2. What is (2 / 3): one user's day today on the spine, baseline and who
   measured it.
3. What could be (1.5 / 2.5): the same day after, labelled "we hypothesise".
4. Three reasons to test now (2 / 4): the complication, the data and access
   in hand, the kill criteria.
5. What could go wrong and the answer (1.5 / 3): top two risks, each with a
   control and owner.
6. Criteria, options, ask (2 / 4): thresholds, "stop / run POC / wider
   pilot", recommendation, decider, date.
7. Q&A, then the close (restate the decision and the date).

### Production approval (15: 11 + 4; 30: 20 + 10)

1. BLUF (1 / 1.5): "The POC met <n of m> criteria; I recommend <option> at
   <cost per month>; the decision is yours by <date>."
2. What we measured (3 / 5): the criteria table spoken, method and n in one
   clause each; the recorded demo of the measured build.
3. One real case (2 / 4): a user before and after, on the spine.
4. What did not work and what changed (2 / 4): failures, counts, fix or
   scope cut.
5. Three reasons to build (1.5 / 2.5): outcome against baseline, cost against
   estimate, workflow and human-validation points.
6. Options, ask, credit (1.5 / 3): stop / extend / build, recommendation,
   decider, date; name the team.
7. Q&A, then the close.

### Final results (15: 10 + 5; 30: 17 + 13)

1. BLUF (1 / 1.5): "<Agent> has run <period>; <metric> is <measured> against
   <baseline>; cost <actual> against <estimate>; <incidents> incidents."
2. The number and its method (2 / 4): time series, n, who measured, the
   counterfactual ("without the agent we estimate <x>, because <reason>").
3. One user's year (2 / 3.5): the spine, including the day something failed.
4. What did not work and what we learned (2 / 3.5).
5. Credit (1 / 1.5): names and what each did, including partners.
6. What is next (2 / 3): options and a recommendation, or "no decision today".
7. Q&A, then the close.

## Rehearsal and delivery checklist

- Throughline is one sentence someone could disagree with; the first spoken
  sentence is the ask and the number.
- Three reasons, spoken as "first, second, third"; extra evidence parked.
- Every number carries method and sample size in the same breath; uncertainty
  is a range, not an adverb; the three voices are kept distinct.
- One "did not work" section with the response, before the ask.
- Demo recorded from the measured build, labelled, with a fallback copy.
- Notes at 140 to 160 words per minute (about 1,500 words for 15 minutes);
  cut, do not speed up.
- Two full runs out loud, one before a colleague who interrupts and plays the
  decider; time each section against the skeleton.
- Pre-wire the decider and one sceptic; update the Q&A bank from their questions.
- Say "I am excited" before entering; pause instead of filling.
- Hype words removed; risk controls phrased as what the agent cannot do and
  the test that proves it; leave-behind and decision memo ready.

## Q&A protocol

1. Let the question finish; pause two beats.
2. Restate it in one neutral sentence so the room hears it.
3. Answer in one sentence; then the evidence (method, n); then restate.
4. Bridge: "which is why the recommendation is <option>."
5. If unknown: "I don't know. I will find out and send it to <name> by
   <date>." Say it aloud; never guess.
6. If hostile: acknowledge, probe ("what would you need to see?"), answer the
   fact, look at the whole room, offer to continue after.
7. If off-topic or unanswerable here: park it with an owner and a date.
8. End with the close, not the last answer: decision, decider, date, and a
   memo within 24 hours listing decisions, parked questions with owners and
   dates, and the next checkpoint.
