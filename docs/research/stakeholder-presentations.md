# Presenting an ADK project to business and technical stakeholders

Primary-source review, 15 October 2026, for the proposed `adk-presenter` skill.
Pages can change after the date read. Books are cited by edition where their
text is not online. This note records what the sources prescribe and what they
measured; it does not establish that any deck structure causes better decisions.

## Conclusion

The sources converge on one discipline: state the answer first, make every slide
title a claim, put the evidence for the claim on the same slide, and put the
decision you want on the first page and the last. The AI research adds a second:
pilots mostly fail to reach production over unclear value, cost, risk controls
and workflow fit, not model quality, so a credible deck shows pre-agreed success
criteria, the measurement method beside every number, and a demo that is the
measured system. Business decks are short, decision-framed and option-based;
technical decks are contract-, evidence- and operations-based. The skill should
generate both from the project's recorded design, plan and test evidence only,
label every hypothesis as such, and never fill a gap with an invented number.

## What the sources actually establish

### 1. Minto: answer first, and find the question before you write

Barbara Minto's site states the core rule: "Your thinking will be easy for a
reader to grasp if you present the ideas organized as a pyramid under a single
point", and names the "Situation, Complication, Question Framework (SCQ
Framework)" as the device "for identifying the question in your reader's mind".
The 1996 edition of *The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking and
Problem Solving* supersedes the 1987 book and is described as a standard text at
the major consulting firms. The site is a course prospectus; it asserts the
method, it does not report an experiment.
[barbaraminto.com](https://www.barbaraminto.com/), read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** open every deck with Situation (what the business does today),
Complication (what changed or hurts), Question (what must be decided) and Answer
(the recommendation), then arrange supporting slides as groups under that answer.
Write the Question explicitly before any slide; if the project record does not
contain the complication, ask the user rather than invent it.

### 2. Consulting slide conventions: action titles, ghost deck, one message

McKinsey, BCG and Bain do not publish their internal slide standards. The
readable practitioner statement is Paul Millerd's StrategyU guide (8 February
2025): "A ghost deck is a preliminary, bare-bones outline of your presentation
where you only include the slide titles and minimal bullet points"; "Each slide
should have a title that states the key takeaway"; "Each slide should communicate
one key insight with supporting data"; plus an executive summary of the
recommendation and rationale. Ethan Rasiel's *The McKinsey Way* (McGraw-Hill,
1999) is the source for pre-wiring, quoted by a secondary summary as "There
should be no surprises on the day of the presentation." Bain's "answer first"
appears only on interview-prep sites; treat it as consistent with Minto.
[StrategyU](https://strategyu.co/consulting-presentations/),
[pre-wiring summary](https://www.rightattitudes.com/2008/05/27/pre-wiring-presentations-for-buy-in),
both read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** the skill produces the ghost deck first: an ordered list of
action titles that reads as the argument on its own, each with the evidence it
needs. Only then are slide bodies drafted. Add a pre-wiring step: list the
decision-makers to brief individually before the meeting.

### 3. Zelazny: message, then comparison, then chart

Gene Zelazny (*Say It With Charts*, 4th edition, McGraw-Hill, 2001) prescribes
three steps: determine the message, identify the comparison it implies, then
select the chart form. The five comparisons are component, item, time series,
frequency distribution and correlation. A practitioner summary adds his view
that bar, column and line charts "cover 80 to 90 percent of everything you need
to show" and that the one-sentence message "becomes your slide title". The
publisher's chapter page returned HTTP 403; the edition is from the catalogue.
[Chapter listing](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/the-say-it/9780071474702/ch02.html),
[StrategyU summary](https://strategyu.co/think-before-you-chart),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** for each evidence slide, name the comparison (for example,
"item: agent vs baseline on task success", "time series: cost per conversation
across the pilot") before choosing the chart. Reject a chart whose message is
not the slide title.

### 4. Amazon: the written narrative and the PR/FAQ as the non-slide option

Bezos's 2017 shareholder letter: "We don't do PowerPoint (or any other
slide-oriented) presentations at Amazon. Instead, we write narratively
structured six-page memos. We silently read one at the beginning of each meeting
in a kind of 'study hall.'" The *Working Backwards* excerpt on Amazon's site
describes the PR/FAQ: a press release "always less than one page", an FAQ "five
pages or less", read silently at the start of the meeting. These describe
Amazon's practice; they do not compare it with slides.
[2017 letter](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2017-letter-to-shareholders),
[Working Backwards excerpt](https://aboutamazon.com/news/workplace/an-insider-look-at-amazons-culture-and-processes),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** offer the POC-approval case as a PR/FAQ when the organisation
reads documents rather than watches decks, and always produce a one-page
narrative leave-behind alongside the deck.

### 5. Duarte: five minutes, summary first, what is at stake

Nancy Duarte's HBR Blog post "How to Present to Senior Executives" (4 October
2012, republished on duarte.com) prescribes: pretend "your whole slot got cut to
5 minutes" and lead with findings, conclusions, recommendations and the call to
action; set expectations that the first minutes are summary and the rest
discussion; apply a 10 percent rule (five summary slides for a fifty-slide
appendix); answer what they asked for before anything else; rehearse with an
experienced colleague. Her Big Idea formula requires "your unique point of view
and what's at stake" in one complete sentence; "If someone could disagree with
the idea, you have a Duarte Big Idea. If not, it's just a topic." These are
practitioner prescriptions, not measured outcomes.
[Duarte on executives](https://www.duarte.com/blog/how-to-effectively-present-to-senior-executives/),
[Big Idea](https://www.duarte.com/blog/how-to-develop-the-best-big-idea-for-your-presentation/),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** every business deck has a five-minute path (title, executive
summary, decision slide) that survives being cut short; the rest is appendix.
The executive summary carries a one-sentence Big Idea with what is at stake.

### 6. Tufte: slides compress evidence, and the Columbia slide

*The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint* (2003; 2nd edition 2006) argues the format
"suffocates evidence and thought with bullet points" and turns "serious
discussions into a sales pitch". The Columbia Accident Investigation Board, on
the Boeing debris slide, wrote: "It is easy to understand how a senior manager
might read this PowerPoint slide and not realize that it addresses a
life-threatening situation." Tufte's remedy is a written technical report or
handout. The essay is an argument with a documented case, not a controlled
study.
[edwardtufte.com](https://www.edwardtufte.com/?p=460), read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** the technical deck links to the written design, contracts and
test report instead of summarising them into bullets; uncertainty qualifiers go
in the title, never in a sub-bullet; risk slides state the consequence in a
full sentence.

### 7. Gartner: why agentic projects get cancelled

Gartner (25 June 2025) predicts "over 40% of agentic AI projects will be
canceled by the end of 2027, due to escalating costs, unclear business value or
inadequate risk controls", and quotes Anushree Verma: "Most agentic AI projects
right now are early stage experiments or proof of concepts that are mostly
driven by hype and are often misapplied." It is a prediction with a stated
rationale, plus a January 2025 webinar poll of 3,412 attendees; it is not an
observed cancellation rate.
[Gartner press release](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** every deck answers the three named cancellation causes
explicitly: a cost slide, a value slide against a baseline, and a risk-controls
slide with an accountable owner.

### 8. MIT NANDA: the pilot-to-production chasm and what the 5 percent did

*The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025* (July 2025; research January
to June 2025; 300 public initiatives reviewed, 52 organisation interviews, 153
leader surveys) reports: "Sixty percent of organizations evaluated such tools,
but only 20 percent reached pilot stage and just 5 percent reached production."
Its research note defines success as outputs that "users or executives have
remarked as causing a marked and sustained productivity and/or P&L impact" and
adds: "These figures are directionally accurate based on individual interviews
rather than official company reporting." Successful buyers "evaluate tools based
on business outcomes rather than software benchmarks". A quoted CIO: "We've seen
dozens of demos this year. Maybe one or two are genuinely useful. The rest are
wrappers or science projects." The widely repeated 95 percent is, by the
report's own account, directional.
[Report PDF (mirror)](https://mlq.ai/media/quarterly_decks/v0.1_State_of_AI_in_Business_2025_Report.pdf),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** judge the POC on business outcomes agreed in advance, not on
benchmark scores; show how the POC was embedded in a real workflow, and admit
where it was not. Never quote the 95 percent as a measured failure rate.

### 9. McKinsey State of AI 2025: scaling, agents, human validation

McKinsey's survey (1,993 participants, 25 June to 29 July 2025; published
November 2025): 88 percent report regular AI use, but most organisations are
still experimenting or piloting and about a third have begun to scale; 23
percent are scaling an agentic system somewhere; "just 39 percent report EBIT
impact at the enterprise level". High performers (more than 5 percent of EBIT
attributable to AI; n = 109) are "nearly three times as likely as others" to
fundamentally redesign workflows, three times as likely to report senior-leader
ownership, and more likely to "have defined processes to determine how and when
model outputs need human validation". These are self-reported correlations from
a relative-weights analysis, not causal findings.
[McKinsey report](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai),
read via a PDF copy, 15 October 2026 (the HTML page timed out).

**Apply here:** the production-approval deck names the workflow change, the
executive sponsor and the human-validation points, presented as practices the
survey associates with value, not as guarantees.

### 10. BCG: 10-20-70

BCG's AI Radar news (26 January 2026) states "10% of AI value comes from
algorithms, 20% from data and technology, and 70% from people and processes."
It is a heuristic BCG uses, not a measured decomposition.
[BCG](https://www.bcg.com/news/26january2026-what-is-driving-ai-value),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** the production scope slide shows adoption, training and process
change as cost and work items, not only engineering.

### 11. Google: evaluation before production, and production guides

The ADK evaluation page says that "deterministic 'pass/fail' assertions are
often unsuitable", that evaluation compares the agent's "actual trajectory to
an expected, or ideal, one" as well as the final response, and: "If you intend
to progress beyond prototype, this is a highly recommended best practice."
Google Cloud's "Five must-have guides to move agents into production" (Addy
Osmani and Shubham Saboo, 6 May 2026) frame readiness as long-running state and
approval workflows, a governance stack, orchestration, interoperability and
evaluation loops: "a misconfigured agent takes bad actions actively." Google's
"From prototype to production" ebook is gated and was not read.
[ADK evaluate](https://adk.dev/evaluate/),
[Five guides](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/developers-practitioners/five-guides-to-building-and-scaling-production-ready-ai-agents),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** technical decks cite the project's evaluation set, pass rates
and trajectory checks by file; the production deck covers approval points,
governance, observability and recovery, each mapped to a recorded decision.

### 12. Klein: the premortem

Gary Klein (HBR, September 2007; his 2021 summary) describes the procedure:
tell the team "The plan you've just discussed has turned out to be a failure",
each person writes reasons for two minutes, the room shares with the leader
first, and showstoppers are compiled. One small study (Veinott, Klein and
Wiggins, 2010) found the premortem "showed the greatest reduction in
(over)confidence" versus critique or pros-and-cons.
[HBR](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem),
[Klein 2021](https://www.psychologytoday.com/ie/blog/seeing-what-others-dont/202101/the-pre-mortem-method),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** run a premortem on the deck: "this presentation failed to get
the decision; why?" The output becomes the Q&A bank and the appendix.

### 13. Bain RAPID: who decides, who recommends

Rogers and Blenko (HBR, January 2006) define Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input,
Decide, and insist "it comes down to one person who must decide, the single
point of accountability who commits the organization to action." The
recommender proposes "a course of action, either by making a proposal or
offering alternatives".
[Bain](https://www.bain.com/insights/who-has-the-d/), read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** the decision slide names the decider, what is being asked, the
options with the recommended one marked, and the date; the follow-up memo
records the decision, the owner of each action and the next checkpoint.

### 14. Options, not a single proposal

Enders, König and Barsoux (MIT Sloan Management Review, Summer 2016) cite Paul
Nutt (Academy of Management Executive, 1999): in seven of ten organisational
decisions studied, "the decision makers considered just one alternative", and
over half of those decisions failed against under a third with two or more
options. This is an observational finding from Nutt's case set, not a trial.
[MIT SMR](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/stop-jumping-to-solutions/),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** every ask is framed as two or three real options (for example,
stop, extend the pilot, build for production at scope X) with a recommendation.

### 15. Ries: vanity metrics

Eric Ries (23 December 2009) argues that vanity metrics such as "hits" let
"each person in the company live in their own private reality", that annotated
charts show "correlation at best, never causation", and that without actionable
data "teams ... are just rolling the dice".
[Startup Lessons Learned](https://www.startuplessonslearned.com/2009/12/why-vanity-metrics-are-dangerous.html),
read 15 October 2026.

**Apply here:** conversation counts and message volume go to the appendix at
most; the headline metric is the outcome agreed at POC approval (resolution
rate, handling time, cost per case, error rate) with its baseline.

### What the sources do not establish

No primary source prescribes "10 to 15 minutes" for executives; Duarte's
five-minute test is the closest. No source prescribes a cost format for agents;
the per-conversation and per-month view below follows from Gartner's
"escalating costs" cause and from how ADK projects record token and hosting
cost. "Demo is not the measured system" is our rule, supported by the MIT CIO
quote and the ADK evaluation guidance rather than stated by either.

## Proposed slide outlines

Each slide is an action-title placeholder plus the evidence it needs. Angle
brackets mark values that must come from the project record; if the record lacks
them the slide says "not yet measured" or the agent asks. Business decks keep a
five-minute path (slides 1 to 3) and end on a decision slide.

### A1. POC approval, business audience (10 slides)

1. Executive summary: "<Process> costs <baseline> today; a <timebox> POC can test whether an agent cuts it by <hypothesis>" (SCQA in four lines; Big Idea with stakes)
2. "Today <process> takes <baseline measure> per <unit>" (baseline source and date)
3. "The complication is <change or pain>" (recorded problem statement)
4. "We propose to test one hypothesis: <hypothesis>" (hypothesis as written in the plan)
5. "Success means <criteria and thresholds>, measured by <method>" (pre-agreed criteria, sample size, who measures)
6. "We will stop early if <kill criteria>" (kill criteria and review date)
7. "The POC costs <budget> over <timebox> and needs <people, data, access>" (plan budget; model and cloud estimate marked estimate)
8. "Risks and how they are contained" (data, security, user harm; owner per risk)
9. "At the end you will see <artefact list>" (measured report, not a demo only)
10. Decision: "Options: do nothing, run the POC, run a wider pilot; we recommend <option>; decider <name>, by <date>"

### A2. POC approval, technical audience (12 slides)

1. Executive summary: "<Agent> is a <architecture> on <ADK/Cloud Run or Agent Runtime>; the POC tests <hypothesis> in <timebox>"
2. "The user and task boundary is <scope>" (in-scope and out-of-scope list)
3. "Architecture: <components>" (recorded design diagram)
4. "Contracts: tools, output schema, session state" (contract table from design)
5. "Data and access: <sources> via <identity>" (data owners, permissions requested)
6. "Evaluation plan: <eval set size> cases, trajectory and response checks" (eval design, graders, thresholds)
7. "Baseline measurement method" (how baseline was or will be measured)
8. "Security and sensitive-data boundaries" (threat list, controls planned)
9. "Cost model: per conversation and per month" (token and hosting assumptions marked estimate)
10. "What we ask of your team" (hours, reviews, environments)
11. "What we will hand back if it stops" (code, eval set, findings)
12. Decision: "We need <access, environment, reviewer> by <date>; owner <name>"

### B1. POC review and production ask, business audience (11 slides)

1. Executive summary: "The POC met <n of m> criteria; we recommend <option> at <cost>"
2. "Criteria agreed on <date> and results" (table: criterion, threshold, measured, method, sample size)
3. "Headline outcome: <metric> moved from <baseline> to <measured>" (chart by comparison type, n shown)
4. "What did not work" (failed criteria, failure modes, counts)
5. "What we learned that changes the plan" (recorded findings)
6. "Production scope: <scope> for <users>" (workflow change named, adoption plan)
7. "Production cost: <per conversation>, <per month>, <build>" (model, cloud, people; estimates labelled)
8. "Risks, controls, owners" (incidents in POC, mitigations, accountable owner)
9. "Human validation points and governance" (where people check outputs; sponsor)
10. "Timeline and checkpoints" (milestones, go/no-go dates)
11. Decision: "Options: stop, extend, build; we recommend <option>; decider <name>, by <date>"

### B2. POC review and production ask, technical audience (13 slides)

1. Executive summary: "The POC passed <n of m> evaluation gates; production needs <changes>"
2. "What was built versus the design" (deviations list)
3. "Evaluation results: <pass rate> on <n> cases, trajectory and response" (eval report link, repeats, variance)
4. "Failure analysis: <top failure modes>" (counts, examples, root causes)
5. "Demo versus measured system" (what the demo shows, what the numbers cover)
6. "Measured latency, tokens and cost per conversation" (traces, sample size)
7. "Production architecture changes" (hosting, memory, auth, guardrails)
8. "Contracts your services will depend on" (tool, API, schema, versioning)
9. "Security, sensitive data and approval points" (controls, test evidence)
10. "Observability, alerts and on-call" (SLIs, dashboards, who is paged)
11. "Release and rollback" (gates, pinned models, rollback of prompt and model)
12. "What your team inherits and the work it needs" (hours, skills, docs)
13. Decision: "We need <owners, environments, reviews> by <date>"

### C1. Final result, business audience (10 slides)

1. Executive summary: "<Agent> is live for <users>; <metric> is <measured> against <baseline>; next is <proposal>"
2. "Outcome against baseline over <period>" (time series with method and n)
3. "Adoption: <users, share of cases>" (usage with definition of active)
4. "Cost: <per conversation>, <per month> versus estimate" (actuals from billing)
5. "Incidents and how they were handled" (count, severity, response)
6. "What changed in the workflow" (process change delivered)
7. "What we would do differently" (recorded lessons)
8. "Credit: the team and partners" (names and roles)
9. "What is next and what it needs" (options)
10. Decision or information: "We ask for <decision> or none; owner <name>"

### C2. Final result, technical audience (12 slides)

1. Executive summary: "Production run <period>: <SLO attainment>, <cost>, <incidents>"
2. "Architecture as shipped" (final diagram, deviations from the plan)
3. "Evaluation and release gates in force" (gate thresholds, last results)
4. "Production quality: <SLIs> over <period>" (monitoring data)
5. "Incidents, root causes, fixes" (postmortem links)
6. "Cost breakdown: model, hosting, people" (actuals)
7. "Security and data boundary status" (controls verified, open items)
8. "Operations handover: runbooks, alerts, on-call" (ownership table)
9. "Known limitations and technical debt" (recorded)
10. "Eval set refresh and model migration plan" (dates)
11. "Credit" (contributors)
12. "Next work and decisions needed"

## Speaker narrative pattern

Open with the Question and the Answer in two sentences, then say how the time
will run (summary first, then discussion). For each slide, speak the action
title, point to the evidence, give its method and sample size in one clause,
and end with the implication for the decision. Keep three voices distinct: "we
measured" for results, "we estimate" for projections, "we hypothesise" for
untested claims. Close with the options, the recommendation, the decider and
the date, and name the leave-behind (one-page summary, decision memo with
owners).

## Q&A question bank

Business: What is the baseline and who measured it? What exactly do you want
from me today? What does it cost per month at full adoption? What happens if
the model price or behaviour changes? Who is accountable when it gives a wrong
answer to a customer? What would make you recommend stopping? Why this scope
and not larger or smaller? How many users and cases is this result based on?
What did not work? Who else has signed off?

Technical: Which evaluation cases cover the worst failure modes, and how many
times were they run? Is the demo the same build as the measured numbers? What
are the tool contracts and who owns changes? How is identity propagated and
what can the agent write? What is the p95 latency and token cost per turn? What
stops a runaway loop or a prompt injection? How do we roll back prompt, model
and schema together? What do we inherit for on-call? Which model version is
pinned and when does it deprecate? Where are the sensitive-data boundaries
tested?

Premortem prompts for the deck: the executive stopped at slide two; a number
had no method; the demo differed from the measured system; the cost could not
be converted to a monthly figure; the risk slide had no owner; the decision was
never asked for.
